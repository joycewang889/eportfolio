Discovery Project

Wheatstone Bridge Sensor Circuit

For my Discovery Project I analyzed a Wheatstone bridge, made it more sensitive with an instrumentation amplifier in LTspice, and built the bridge on a breadboard to compare real measurements with my calculations.

Tools LTspice, Keysight E3630A supply, Keithley DMM6500 Built at the Hive Makerspace Note breadboard is the passive bridge only; the amplifier was simulated

Problem

A lot of sensors work by changing resistance. A thermistor changes with temperature, a strain gauge with stretch, and a photoresistor with light. The problem is that the change is usually tiny compared to the total resistance.

If you put a 1 kΩ sensor in a voltage divider with 5 V, the middle sits at about 2.5 V, and a 1 Ω change only moves it about 1.25 mV. Seeing 1.25 mV on top of 2.5 V is like trying to weigh a feather sitting on a bowling ball.

A Wheatstone bridge puts two dividers side by side and measures between their middles. When both sides split the voltage the same way, the output is 0 V, so when the sensor changes you only see the change. I wanted to understand why that works, find where it falls short, and make a version that's easier to read.

Process

1. Deriving the output

I found Vout two ways, with voltage division and with nodal analysis (KCL at nodes C and D), and got the same answer:

Vout = Vc − Vd = Vs · [ R2/(R1+R2) − R4/(R3+R4) ]

The bridge is balanced (Vout = 0) when R1/R2 = R3/R4. Vs isn't in that condition, so a balanced bridge still reads zero if the supply drifts a little.

2. Researching where it's used

I looked at three real uses. Digital scales use load cells with four strain gauges in a full bridge, and a typical one only outputs about 2 mV per volt of supply. Mass airflow sensors in cars put a heated wire in the bridge and use feedback to keep it balanced, so the power needed shows how much air is flowing. Hospital blood pressure transducers use resistors on a thin silicon diaphragm and all share one standard sensitivity (5 µV/V/mmHg), so any sensor works with any monitor. In all three, the output is tiny and anything connected to it can load it down.

3. Making it active

Looking back into C and D, the bridge acts like a source with about 1 kΩ behind it, so connecting a 10 kΩ load loses about 9% of the signal. I added an AD8221 instrumentation amplifier, which has very high input impedance and amplifies only the difference between C and D. Op-amp buffers would fix loading but have a gain of 1, and a basic difference amp would load the bridge through its input resistors.

G = 1 + 49.4 kΩ / RG,   RG = 499 Ω  →  G ≈ 100

The handout suggested a gain of 1000, but with ±15 V supplies the output tops out around 14 V, so 1000 would clip after about a 10 Ω change. I used 100 so most of my test range would fit.

5. Building the bridge

At the Hive I built the passive bridge on a breadboard with 1 kΩ resistors for R1, R3 and R4 and swapped different resistors into R2. A Keysight E3630A supplied 4.99 V and I measured Vout between C and D with a Keithley DMM6500.

6. Picking a sensor

I picked a GL5528 photoresistor. Its datasheet puts it around 10 to 20 kΩ in room light and about 1 MΩ in the dark, so I planned 10 kΩ legs with a 10 kΩ pot for R4 to balance it. Going from bright to dim light should swing the output from about −0.83 V to +0.83 V. That's already big, so a gain of 100 would saturate right away, and a gain around 2 to 3 (RG of 25 to 49.9 kΩ) would fit much better.

Outcome

The passive simulation matched my hand calculations within 0.3%. The amplifier raised the sensitivity from 1.249 mV/Ω to 125.1 mV/Ω, almost exactly the 100x I designed for, and V(C)−V(D) didn't change with the amp connected, so it wasn't loading the bridge.

The simulation also showed real limits. The amp added about 7.6 mV of offset at the output, which is a 61% error for the 0.1 Ω case but under 0.4% for bigger changes. Measuring at balance and subtracting it, like hitting tare on a scale, would fix that. At 2000 Ω the output saturated at 14.11 V instead of the ideal 83 V.

On the breadboard, the 2000 Ω case was within 0.5% of my calculation, but the small changes were way off. The 1001 Ω case should have read 1.25 mV and read 16.2 mV. The most likely cause is resistor tolerance. When I worked it out, 5% resistors can throw a "balanced" bridge off by up to 250 mV, which buries a signal of a few millivolts. A big change is large enough to stand out, which is why 2000 Ω still matched. Breadboard contact resistance and long jumper wires add more error.

What I took away: measure the change instead of the total, pick the gain based on how much the sensor actually moves, and plan for calibration from the start. A better version would use precision resistors, a trim pot to balance the bridge, and a PCB instead of a breadboard. Next I'd like to build the photoresistor version with a pot and then build the amplifier stage for real with a lower gain.
